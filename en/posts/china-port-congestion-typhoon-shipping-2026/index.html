China Port Congestion After Typhoon Disruptions: What Shippers Should Watch

Shanghai and Ningbo face vessel backlogs and schedule disruption after severe weather. Learn how shippers can manage rollover, blank sailing and transit risks.

2026-08-21 6 min read Share

Key Takeaways

Severe weather disruption in East China has created another reminder that port congestion can continue well after a storm itself has passed.

During the August 12–18 period, Shanghai and Ningbo were dealing with vessel backlogs, elevated yard utilization and schedule instability following Typhoon Dolphin. Carriers were also omitting selected port calls, increasing the risk of cargo rollover and unpredictable transit times.

For importers and exporters, the key issue is not simply whether a port is technically open. The operational backlog left behind by a disruption can affect vessel schedules, container availability and inland transportation for days afterward.

What Is Happening at Shanghai and Ningbo?

Shanghai and Ningbo-Zhoushan are two of the most important container gateways in global trade. Disruption at these ports can therefore affect supply chains well beyond China.

According to recent port operational updates, Typhoon Dolphin disrupted East China supply chains and contributed to vessel backlogs at Shanghai and Ningbo.

Elevated container-yard utilization and delayed vessel arrivals have also increased schedule instability as terminals and carriers work through accumulated cargo.

Some carriers have omitted selected port calls, creating additional risks for cargo already booked on affected services.

Why Congestion Can Continue After a Typhoon

A common mistake is to assume that shipping operations immediately return to normal once severe weather has passed.

In reality, temporary port closures or navigation restrictions can create a queue of vessels waiting to berth.

Once operations resume, terminals must process delayed vessels while also handling ships arriving according to their original schedules.

This can result in vessel bunching, higher yard utilization and pressure on trucking and equipment availability.

The result is a recovery period that may last considerably longer than the original weather disruption.

Five Risks Shippers Should Monitor

1. Vessel Schedule Changes

Estimated departure and arrival times can change repeatedly while carriers reorganize vessel rotations.

Shippers should monitor the actual vessel schedule rather than relying only on the original booking confirmation.

2. Cargo Rollover

When capacity becomes constrained, containers may be moved to a later sailing.

This is commonly known as a rollover and can add several days or more to the shipment schedule depending on service frequency and available capacity.

3. Port Omission

Carriers may temporarily skip a scheduled port call to recover vessel schedules.

If this happens, cargo may need to move through an alternative port or wait for another vessel.

4. Equipment Availability

Schedule disruption can interfere with normal container repositioning.

Exporters should therefore confirm equipment availability, particularly when specific container types are required.

5. Inland Transportation Delays

Port congestion does not remain inside the terminal.

Truck appointments, container pickup and delivery schedules can also become more difficult when large volumes of delayed cargo move through the port at the same time.

Blank Sailings Can Add Another Layer of Risk

Weather disruption is not the only capacity issue shippers currently need to consider.

Blank sailings remain an important capacity-management tool across major container trades.

A growing global container fleet therefore does not automatically mean that every trade lane will have abundant usable capacity at all times.

When blank sailings coincide with port congestion, shippers can face fewer available departures precisely when delayed cargo is trying to move.

This combination can increase rollover risk and make transit times less predictable.

What Importers and Exporters Should Do

Confirm the Actual Vessel Schedule

Check the latest ETD, ETA and vessel rotation with the carrier or freight forwarder shortly before cargo cutoff.

Check for Port Omissions

Confirm whether the booked vessel is still scheduled to call at the intended origin and destination ports.

Ask About Rollover Risk

If a shipment is time-sensitive, ask whether space is confirmed and whether the service is currently experiencing significant rollover.

Build Additional Lead Time

A schedule that normally works with minimal buffer may become vulnerable during a congestion recovery period.

Critical shipments should therefore include additional time for possible vessel and terminal delays.

Review Alternative Gateways

For supply chains with sufficient flexibility, alternative Chinese gateways may provide another option when congestion becomes severe.

However, changing ports can introduce additional trucking costs, documentation changes and new capacity constraints, so alternatives should be evaluated based on total landed cost rather than ocean freight alone.

How to Evaluate Whether a Shipment Is at Risk

Before cargo is delivered to the terminal, shippers can use a simple operational checklist:

  • Has the vessel's ETD changed?
  • Has the vessel rotation changed?
  • Is the origin port still included in the service?
  • Has the carrier announced a blank sailing?
  • Is container equipment available?
  • Has the cargo cutoff changed?
  • Is the shipment vulnerable to rollover?
  • Are destination connections still achievable?

For transshipment cargo, the final question is particularly important. A delay at the origin port can cause the container to miss its planned connecting vessel at the transshipment hub.

Why This Matters Beyond China

Shanghai and Ningbo handle cargo connected to supply chains across Asia, Europe and North America.

Schedule disruption in East China can therefore propagate through vessel rotations and affect later port calls even after local congestion begins to improve.

This is why shippers should monitor not only the status of an individual port but also the wider service rotation used by their cargo.

Conclusion

Typhoon-related disruption at major Chinese ports illustrates an important supply-chain principle: a port reopening does not necessarily mean that the logistics network has returned to normal.

Vessel backlogs, high yard utilization, port omissions and equipment imbalances can continue to affect shipments during the recovery period.

Importers and exporters using Shanghai or Ningbo should therefore monitor actual vessel schedules, blank sailings, rollover risk and inland transportation conditions before relying on the original transit plan.

For time-sensitive cargo, building additional schedule buffer and discussing alternative routing with carriers or freight forwarders can reduce the risk of an unexpected supply-chain interruption.

FAQ

Are Shanghai and Ningbo ports closed?

The recent issue is better understood as operational disruption and backlog rather than simply a continuing full port closure. Conditions can change quickly, so shippers should verify current terminal and vessel status before each shipment.

What is cargo rollover?

Rollover occurs when booked cargo does not move on its originally planned vessel and is transferred to a later sailing.

What is a blank sailing?

A blank sailing occurs when a carrier cancels a scheduled voyage or removes a sailing from a service rotation. It can temporarily reduce available shipping capacity.

Can port congestion affect cargo after it leaves China?

Yes. Delayed departures can affect connecting vessels at transshipment hubs and disrupt later port calls within the same vessel rotation.

Should shippers change from Shanghai or Ningbo to another port?

Not automatically. Alternative gateways can help in some cases, but trucking costs, available capacity, documentation requirements and the new route's schedule should all be considered.

How often should shipment schedules be checked during congestion?

Time-sensitive shipments should be monitored more frequently than under normal conditions, particularly around cargo cutoff, vessel departure and any planned transshipment connection.
